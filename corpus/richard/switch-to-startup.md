# Richard Lawrence - "switch to enterprise" (moving from enterprise software sales to a startup)

Source: Google Doc 1dlunfHnsLgySApR9lrjy1ybFn6_WtbEkQrIGZIhafEE (owner: Richard Lawrence), fetched 2026-09-30.
Use: Richard's personal voice. Credibility: career selling databases and data integration (Talend, Pentaho, StreamSets).

One of the biggest changes in my life was moving from selling enterprise software to a startup where the people building the product had lived the problems we were trying to solve.

I've spent my career in data, selling databases and data integration technologies, including Talend, Pentaho and StreamSets. Along the way, I came to understand how much depends on getting data right. Behind every technical discussion was a business trying to make better decisions, serve its customers or get something done faster.

That was the part of selling I enjoyed most: understanding a problem and helping someone solve it.

But over time, I began to feel a growing frustration.

In some of the larger software businesses I worked in, the distance between the people building the product and the customers using it seemed to grow as the company expanded. There were talented developers doing good work, but they didn't always have enough direct contact with customers to understand what mattered most to them.

New features would arrive, and the sales team would be expected to sell them. Sometimes they were exactly what a customer needed. Other times, I struggled to see how they addressed the customer's priorities.

Yet there was still a target to hit.

A new version created an opportunity to charge more. An account became an expansion opportunity. Once a deal appeared on the forecast, the pressure to close it could start to dominate the conversation. I found that increasingly uncomfortable, particularly when I couldn't clearly connect the sale to a better outcome for the customer.

Those frustrations helped shape my move into a startup.

What appealed to me was working alongside people with deep, practical experience in data engineering. They understood the work because they had done it themselves. They knew where projects became difficult, where time was wasted and where existing tools fell short.

That gave me a stronger connection to the product and the reasons for building it. I could see how the team's experience related directly to the challenges customers were describing.

Commercial success still matters. Every business needs it. But I want that success to come from customers seeing real value in what they buy.

For me, this change brought me closer to the work I've always found most rewarding: listening to a customer, understanding what they need and helping them move their business forward.
